# Policy Robustness

The current behavior cloning policy can pick the cube, then drop it before placement and drive the arm into unstable motions. The main cause is closed-loop distribution shift: after small prediction errors, the policy enters states that are not represented in the single demonstration and begins extrapolating poor arm targets.

## Immediate Safety Guards

- Clamp predicted arm and gripper commands to MuJoCo actuator control ranges before writing `data.ctrl`.
- Limit the maximum per-step joint target delta so one bad prediction cannot create a large target jump.
- Stop or pause rollout when predicted controls are far outside the demonstrated action range.
- Log policy actions, raw gripper output, cube position, tray position, grasp position, and actuator-range violations during inference.

These guards do not make the policy correct, but they make failures inspectable instead of explosive.

## Data Robustness

- Train on more than one trajectory. A single `.npz` teaches only one narrow expert path.
- Collect demonstrations with randomized cube position, tray position, initial arm perturbations, controller timing, and grasp/hover offsets where possible.
- Prefer tens of successful episodes at minimum; hundreds are better.
- Add recovery data by running the learned policy, letting it drift, switching the expert back on, and saving expert corrective actions for those off-trajectory states.

Recovery data is especially important because it teaches the policy what to do after it makes small closed-loop mistakes.

## Task Phase Or Memory

The expert controller has explicit phases, but the MLP must infer task stage only from geometry. That is fragile because similar poses can require different actions depending on whether the cube has already been grasped or placed.

Useful options:

- Add a one-hot expert phase label to the observation during data collection.
- Use a short observation/action history window.
- Use a recurrent policy such as a GRU or LSTM.

The simplest first version is a one-hot phase vector if data is generated by the scripted expert.

## Gripper Modeling

The gripper is currently treated as a continuous MSE target and thresholded during inference. A small prediction error near the threshold can open the gripper early and drop the cube.

Better options:

- Train arm actions with MSE and gripper state with binary classification loss.
- Output a gripper logit and use `BCEWithLogitsLoss`.
- Add hysteresis during inference, for example close above a high threshold and open only below a low threshold.
- Keep the previous gripper command when confidence is ambiguous.

## Action Representation

Directly predicting absolute joint targets makes bad predictions dangerous. More stable alternatives are:

- Predict clipped joint deltas relative to current `qpos`.
- Predict end-effector pose deltas and use IK to produce joint targets.
- Smooth absolute joint targets before applying them.

For this project, clipped joint deltas are likely the lowest-effort robust improvement.

## Closed-Loop Evaluation

Do not rely only on supervised train loss. A model can have low teacher-forced error and still fail during rollout.

Track these rollout metrics headlessly:

- First actuator-range violation timestep.
- Maximum actuator-range violation magnitude.
- Maximum joint velocity.
- Whether the cube was lifted.
- Whether the cube dropped before reaching the tray.
- Final cube-to-tray XY error.
- Gripper switch timesteps.

## Recommended Order

1. Enable actuator clamping and per-step action smoothing in inference.
2. Add rollout logging for policy actions and range violations.
3. Collect 20-50 varied successful demonstrations.
4. Retrain and validate with closed-loop rollout metrics.
5. Change gripper prediction from MSE regression to binary classification.
6. Add phase input or short observation history.
7. Add DAgger-style recovery data.

The highest-impact fix is more varied recovery data. The fastest safety fix is action clamping plus per-step smoothing.
